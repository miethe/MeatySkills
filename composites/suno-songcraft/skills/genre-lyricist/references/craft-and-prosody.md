# Lyric craft and prosody

This is a practical, authored workflow. Its broad grounding is the relationship between message, structure, rhythm, and emotional purpose discussed in L1–L3 of [sources.md](sources.md). The examples and tests below are original heuristics, not universal genre laws.

## Purpose before rhyme

Write the song's tension as a sentence with an agent and a choice. “A person returns home” is a setting; “A person who always leaves decides to stay through the argument” contains movement. A meditative song need not contain plot, but it should have a reason for repetition, stasis, or fragmentation.

Define the narrator's relationship to the addressee. Is “you” a lover, home, younger self, audience, God, or intentionally more than one? Intentional ambiguity needs internal coherence; accidental switching does not become depth simply because it is unexplained.

## Section economics

Give each section a task. A verse can introduce an event or sensory evidence; a chorus can name the emotional claim; a bridge can alter the angle or stakes. These are options, not mandatory positions in every form. A repeated chorus can acquire new meaning from a changed verse without changing a word.

Before drafting, summarize each proposed section in five to ten words. If two summaries are the same, decide whether the repetition is purposeful. Cut the second section or make it advance the meaning rather than inventing synonyms.

## Hook construction

Test a small set of hook concepts across different mechanisms: direct confession, concrete image, reversal, question, command, or compact paradox. Do not generate a dozen rhyme-neighbor versions and call them distinct concepts.

A useful hook test asks: Can the listener remember its sound? Does the phrase express the actual conflict? Can it bear repetition? Is there a singable vowel at the likely held note? Does it still work without the production doing all the emotional labor?

An approved hook has priority over the agent's desire to improve everything. Offer a changed hook only as a separate authorized exploration.

## Images as evidence

Use verbs and objects that reveal a relationship. Instead of “I was lonely in the darkness,” an original line such as “I left your porch light on till it burned out” suggests an action, time, and consequence. It may still be wrong for a specific speaker; choose details from the brief, not a universal imagery bank.

Limit image systems enough to preserve coherence. Rain, drought, washing, and returning water can interact if their logic is clear. Bringing in fire, shipwreck, chains, galaxies, and shattered mirrors may dilute that system. Mixed metaphor is allowed when it creates purposeful meaning; do not preserve accidental confusion as sophistication.

Keep factual biography separate from imagined scenes. A regional song does not require named towns, poverty, a pickup truck, church attendance, or a particular family history. Use those only when they belong to the speaker and request.

## Speech stress and musical stress

Prosody concerns how expressive elements support the song's meaning; word-stress alignment is one practical part of it [L1]. Say a line in the voice of the narrator. Mark its naturally emphasized words in a working note, not in the copy-ready lyric.

For a supplied melody, inspect syllable placement, pickup notes, long notes, rests, and phrase peaks. For no melody, propose a stress pattern and keep it provisional. Equal syllable counts do not guarantee equal singability: consonant clusters, vowel length, emphasis, and rests can change the phrase.

Example working analysis:

- Natural meaning: “I **left** the **light** on.”
- A setting that stresses “the” more than “light” may weaken that meaning unless the contrast is intentional.
- A longer replacement may need a pickup or an elision; do not declare it a drop-in fit without the tune.

Treat syllable estimates as language- and performance-dependent. Do not run an English vowel-group counter over Japanese, Spanish, tonal-language, or multilingual lyrics and present it as scansion. In a tonal language, investigate pronunciation and melodic interaction when material; do not claim a generic Western stress test resolves it.

## Breath and mouth feel

Group lines by performed phrases, not solely grammar. Dense verses can work if the groove supports them; sparse hooks can still be awkward if they jam consonants at the peak.

Check the ending vowel of a sustained hook, word boundaries that might blur, repeated plosives or sibilants, and whether a singer gets a real breath before a high-intensity phrase. A fragile spoken delivery and a full belt have different needs. Do not specify physiology or claim a singer can safely perform a range from a text prompt.

## Rhyme with purpose

Choose rhyme after knowing what the line must say. Exact rhyme can provide closure; looser rhyme, assonance, or an unrhymed line can leave tension. These are expressive options rather than a score that all good songs maximize.

Map section rhyme lightly: AABB, ABAB, ABCB, internal rhyme, or intentionally free. Vary density between verse and hook when contrast helps. Avoid forced syntax, filler end words, factual changes made for rhyme, and conspicuously repeated rhymes that draw attention away from the song.

For rap or dense spoken delivery, plan internal stress and rhyme placement against the beat, not just line-ending pairs. For melismatic singing, fewer syllables may carry longer phrases, but do not assume every soul or R&B song needs runs.

## Final read

Read once for meaning without music, once in the intended rhythm, and once checking only the hook. If actual performance is unavailable, report a textual review. Leave uncertain melody-fit issues as precise questions: “Does this extra unstressed syllable fit before the downbeat?” is useful; “Should sing great” is not validation.
